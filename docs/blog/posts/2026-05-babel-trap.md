# The Babel Trap

We have explored how modern AI systems describe the world, reproduce its patterns, and compress its structure into increasingly powerful internal models. Each stage deepens capability, and with it, the expectation that understanding will emerge. It is tempting to believe that, beyond some threshold of scale and integration, these limitations dissolve — that sufficiently capable systems will simply emerge into a more complete grasp of the world.

But if our world is learned through language, whose world is it?

<!-- more -->

Language is not an agnostic encoding of the world. It is a product of human practices: shaped by history, structured by conventions, and saturated with implicit assumptions. It carries with it categories, metaphors, exclusions, and asymmetries that are not incidental to its use, but constitutive of it.

A system trained on large-scale human corpora does not encounter language in the contexts in which it is lived. It encounters it as data. In doing so, it inevitably absorbs the structure of that data — not just its content, but the patterns through which that content has been expressed.

Typically, such a bias is framed as an engineering problem: skewed datasets produce skewed outputs, and better curation or filtering might mitigate the issue. While this is true to an extent, it does not fully capture what is at stake. There is in fact a need for a deeper philosophical reading.

### Meaning Is Use

In *Philosophical Investigations*, Ludwig Wittgenstein argues that the meaning of a word is not determined by an internal representation or a fixed correspondence to objects in the world, but by its use within a form of life. Words acquire their significance through participation in shared practices — through the ways they are deployed, corrected, contested, and sustained within a community.

To understand a word, on this view, is not simply to associate it with a definition. It is to grasp how it functions within a language-game: when it is appropriate, what counts as a misuse, and what commitments follow from employing it in a particular way.

Meaning, therefore, is not merely encoded by words. It is enacted.

A language model, however, encounters none of this directly. It ingests vast quantities of utterances, but those utterances are detached from the situations in which they were produced. The model learns statistical regularities in how words tend to follow one another, but it does so without participating in the practices that give those regularities their normative force.

It absorbs the map of our language-games without playing them.

### Inherited Ontology

Because language reflects how a society partitions and interprets the world, a system trained on language inherits those partitions. The categories through which we describe race, gender, profession, intelligence, or normality are not neutral; they are historically contingent and often unevenly distributed.

This inheritance is not accidental. It is a direct consequence of the training objective. If a model is optimised to predict tokens based on their frequency and context in human-generated text, then the most dominant patterns in that text will exert the strongest influence on the model’s internal representations.

Marginal perspectives become statistically attenuated. Blind spots persist. Dominant frameworks become central attractors. The system comes to reflect the world as it has been described, with all the asymmetries that such descriptions entail. And yet, this reflection can give the impression of something more.

The model appears culturally embedded. It speaks in registers that feel appropriate. It reproduces distinctions that seem socially informed. But we know that this appearance is misleading. The system does not stand within a community in which its words can bind or offend, oblige or liberate. It does not incur commitments through its utterances. It does not negotiate meaning over time, nor does it revise its understanding through lived interaction with others.

It mirrors the structure of our practices without inhabiting them. 

### The Anthropomorphic Frame

Because the data is human-generated, the resulting representation of the world is implicitly anthropocentric. Objects, events, and relations are organised around what matters to human observers — their needs, their concerns, their modes of attention. The world that emerges in the model is therefore not just structured, but structured for us.

Relevance is defined in human terms. Salience tracks human interests. Even apparently neutral descriptions carry traces of what is noticeable, memorable, or important within human experience.

A mountain is described in terms of scale, beauty, or difficulty. A city in terms of function, density, or culture. A tool in terms of its use. The world is filtered through human purposes. The model reproduces a world already organised around a point of view, but without that point of view being its own.

### The Illusion of Fluency

This gives rise to what might be called the Babel Trap.

The model speaks across domains with remarkable fluency. It can shift between technical discourse, casual conversation, policy language, and literary expression with ease. It can translate, summarise, and generate in ways that suggest a deep familiarity with multiple forms of life.

But this multiplicity is combinatorial rather than communal. Fluency, in this case, does not imply understanding in the fuller sense. It indicates alignment with patterns of use, not participation in the practices that sustain them.

This reframes the problem of bias.

Bias is not merely an artefact to be corrected through better engineering. It is a symptom of a more fundamental condition: the system’s “understanding” is derivative. It inherits structures of categorisation without sharing in the processes through which those structures are justified, contested, or transformed.

It cannot step outside the ontology it has absorbed. It cannot renegotiate meaning from within a lived context. It can only be retrained on different distributions.

And this leaves open a question that the earlier discussion only begins to address: whether grounding these inherited structures in perception and action would be sufficient to transform them into something more than inherited form.
