While autoregressive models generate data sequentially, **Latent Variable Models** take a different approach. They assume that the observed data $\mathbf{x}$ is generated by some underlying, unobserved (latent) factors $\mathbf{z}$. The goal is to capture these high-level features in a low-dimensional latent space, which can then be used to generate new data.

<!-- more -->

## The Latent Variable Motivation

The core idea is that complex high-dimensional data (like images) can be described by a smaller set of latent factors (like object type, orientation, colour).

- We assume a prior distribution $p(\mathbf{z})$ over latent variables.
- We model the generation process $p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})$, which maps latent factors to data.

A simple example is a **Mixture of Gaussians**{: .rust}, where $\mathbf{z}$ is a categorical variable selecting a cluster, and $\mathbf{x}$ is sampled from that cluster's Gaussian. A **Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE)**{: .rust} extends this to an "infinite" mixture of Gaussians, where $\mathbf{z}$ is a continuous vector (typically $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$), and the mapping to $\mathbf{x}$ is a non-linear neural network.

## The Challenge of Generation

To train such a model, we want to maximise the likelihood of the data $p(\mathbf{x})$. However, computing this requires integrating over all possible values of $\mathbf{z}$:

$$
p(\mathbf{x}) = \int p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z}) d\mathbf{z}
$$

For complex neural networks, this integral is **intractable**. We cannot easily compute it, nor its gradients. In practical latent spaces like those used for images we encounter the curse of dimensionality, for instance $\mathbf{z}\in \mathbb{R}^{100}$ and the problem becomes one of large nested integrals.

A second challenge when thinking of "encoding" the training samples onto a latent space is the problem of overfitting. Conventional encoders are designed for reconstruction rather than generation and need not concern about random sampling from the latent space.

### Variational Inference and ELBO

To solve these, VAEs introduce an approximate posterior distribution $q_\phi(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})$ (the **encoder**) to approximate the true posterior $p(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})$. Instead of maximising the log-likelihood directly, we maximise a lower bound known as the **Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)**{: .rust}:

$$
\text{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}[\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})] - D_{KL}(q_\phi(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}) || p(\mathbf{z}))
$$

This objective has two intuitive terms:

1.  **Reconstruction Term**{: .rust}: $\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})]$ encourages the model to accurately reconstruct the input $\mathbf{x}$ from the latent code $\mathbf{z}$.
2.  **Regularisation Term**{: .rust}: $-D_{KL}(q || p)$ forces the learned latent distribution $q(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})$ to stay close to the prior $p(\mathbf{z})$ (usually a standard normal distribution).

## Rate distortion objective

The cleanest way to look at the ELBO is as a **rate-distortion objective**{: .rust} from Information Theory.

The first term (negative) in the ELBO is the **Distortion term**{: .rust}, which measures how well the decoder can predict $\mathbf{x}$ from $\mathbf{z}$. In information theoretic terms the log term $-\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})$ is the number of nats required to encode x in latent space. If this was the only term we tried to optimise the resultant latent space would be a series of disjoint and narrow gaussians, each overfitting to a training sample.

The second term is the **Rate term**{: .rust} which measures the KL Divergence between the encoder $q_\phi(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})$ and the prior $p(\mathbf{z})$. Minimising this term keeps the encoder output close to the prior, thereby preventing overfitting and giving generation possibilities across the latent space. This term acts both as an information bottleneck as well as a latent-space regularisation parameter.

Operating on this rate-distortion trade-off is what enables VAEs to balance the cost of encoding with the faithfulness of reconstruction.

## Amortised Inference

In classical variational inference, we would optimise a separate variational parameter for each data point. VAEs use **amortised inference**{: .rust}, where a single neural network (the encoder) learns to map *any* input $\mathbf{x}$ to its approximate posterior parameters (mean and variance).

This allows VAEs to be trained efficiently on large datasets using **Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)**, simultaneously learning how to encode data into a meaningful latent space and how to decode it back into realistic samples.

## Visualisation

The encoded latent-space representation can be visualised as a large mixture of gaussians, one for each training sample, each gets pulled to its own unique co-ordinates in latent space ($\mu_x, \sigma_x)$ by the distortion term and towards the prior $p(\mathbf{z})$ and regularisation due to the KL divergence term.
